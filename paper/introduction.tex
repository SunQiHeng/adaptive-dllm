\section{Introduction}
Large language models (LLMs) have revolutionized natural language processing, yet their dominant autoregressive paradigm incurs substantial latency and computational cost during inference because tokens must be generated sequentially. Diffusion language models (DLMs), also referred to as diffusion-based large language models (DLLMs), offer a compelling alternative by replacing next-token decoding with iterative denoising over partially masked sequences. By refining multiple positions in parallel, DLLMs expose a different accuracy--latency trade-off through the number of denoising steps and the computation allocated to each step. However, because this generation paradigm differs fundamentally from autoregressive decoding, the functional roles of internal components in DLLMs remain poorly understood. Improving that understanding is important not only for interpretability, but also for practical efficiency optimizations during DLLM inference.

Within DLLMs, multi-head attention is the main mechanism for integrating information across token positions during denoising. Identifying which attention heads are important is useful for at least two reasons. First, it improves interpretability by revealing which internal components consistently support reconstruction under noisy conditions. Second, it enables downstream optimizations such as adaptive sparse attention and head pruning, where computation can be preferentially allocated to heads that matter most. In DLLMs, this question is especially natural because the model must repeatedly reconstruct masked tokens under varying masking rates and random masking patterns, raising the question of \emph{which heads remain important across denoising regimes}.

Quantifying such \emph{global} head importance in DLLMs is nontrivial, and existing attribution methods for autoregressive LLMs do not transfer directly. Most prior methods are designed around next-token prediction, whereas DLLMs are trained and evaluated with masked reconstruction objectives defined over multiple masked positions, multiple masking rates, and multiple random masking patterns. As a result, DLLM head attribution faces three practical difficulties: the attribution target is \emph{non-local}, since one forward pass jointly influences many masked positions; the supervision signal is an \emph{expectation over stochastic masks} and masking rates, making naive gradients high-variance; and the underlying corruption process is \emph{discrete}, which further increases sensitivity to particular masking realizations. These differences call for an attribution framework that is aligned with DLLM reconstruction loss and robust to masking variability.

To address these issues, we propose a loss-based head attribution framework, \emph{Head Integrated Gradients} (HeadIG), tailored to diffusion-style masking and denoising. HeadIG introduces a differentiable scaling gate for each attention head and quantifies head importance by integrating gradients of the masked reconstruction loss as the gates transition from a baseline configuration to the full model. Crucially, the objective is averaged over multiple masking probabilities and masking samples, making the attribution procedure match the multi-step, stochastic nature of DLLM denoising rather than a single deterministic prediction setting. We further study two integration paths in the high-dimensional head-gate space: a deterministic \emph{Diagonal Path}, and a more robust \emph{Stochastic Threshold Path} (STP), which reduces sensitivity to any single path by sampling head-wise activation thresholds.

We validate the resulting head-importance scores in two causal ways that connect attribution to both efficiency and behavior. First, we use the scores to drive \emph{adaptive sparse attention}, reallocating sparse budgets toward important heads during inference. Second, we conduct \emph{head masking/pruning} studies, comparing pruning the most important versus least important heads, with random pruning as a control, to test whether the attribution ranking predicts functional necessity. Overall, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a DLLM-aligned, loss-based head attribution framework based on integrated gradients (HeadIG);
    \item a DLLM-suited attribution procedure that averages over masking probabilities and stochastic masking patterns, together with two integration paths for head gating, including the Stochastic Threshold Path (STP);
    \item two complementary validations of head importance via adaptive sparse attention and importance-based head masking/pruning;
    \item empirical evidence across two representative DLLM families and multiple tasks.
\end{itemize}
