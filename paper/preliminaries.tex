    \section{Preliminaries: Diffusion Language Models}
    \label{sec:preliminaries}

    Diffusion Language Models (DLMs), also referred to as diffusion-based large language models (DLLMs), represent a paradigm shift from conventional autoregressive language modeling approaches. This section formalizes the basic notation for DLMs and their attention mechanisms.

    \subsection{Attention Mechanism}

    We consider a transformer-based DLLM with $N$ layers and $H$ attention heads per layer. Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d}$ denote the input hidden states of one layer, where $L$ is the sequence length and $d$ is the model dimension. Each attention head has dimension $d_h = d / H$. Unlike autoregressive LLMs, DLLMs use \emph{bidirectional} attention during denoising, so each token can attend to all other tokens in the sequence rather than only to its left context.

    For head $h \in \{1,\dots,H\}$, the query, key, and value matrices are
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{Q}_h = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_h^Q, \qquad
        \mathbf{K}_h = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_h^K, \qquad
        \mathbf{V}_h = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{W}_h^V,
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{W}_h^Q, \mathbf{W}_h^K, \mathbf{W}_h^V \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_h}$ are the projection matrices for head $h$, and $\mathbf{Q}_h,\mathbf{K}_h,\mathbf{V}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times d_h}$. The attention output of head $h$ is then
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{O}_h = \mathbf{A}_h \mathbf{V}_h
        = \operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}_h \mathbf{K}_h^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right)\mathbf{V}_h,
        \label{eq:attention}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{A}_h \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times L}$ denotes the attention-weight matrix for head $h$.

    The multi-head attention output is obtained by concatenating the outputs of all heads and applying an output projection:
    \begin{equation}
        \operatorname{MultiHead}(\mathbf{X}) =
        \operatorname{Concat}(\mathbf{O}_1,\dots,\mathbf{O}_H)\mathbf{W}^O,
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathbf{W}^O \in \mathbb{R}^{Hd_h \times d}$ is the output projection matrix. This bidirectional attention mechanism enables DLLMs to exploit global context at each denoising step, while different heads may specialize in different reconstruction behaviors. This head-level specialization makes attention-head attribution a natural tool for understanding and optimizing DLLMs.

    \subsection{Core Framework}

    Diffusion Large Language Models (DLLMs) define a model distribution $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)$ through two complementary stochastic processes: a \textit{forward process} that progressively corrupts data, and a \textit{reverse process} that iteratively denoises and recovers the original sequence.

    Given a sequence $\mathbf{x}_0 = (x_{0,1}, \dots, x_{0,L}) \in \mathcal{V}^L$, where $\mathcal{V}$ is the vocabulary and $L$ is the sequence length, the forward process gradually introduces noise by replacing tokens with $\texttt{[MASK]}$ according to a masking schedule. For $t \in [0,1]$, the corruption distribution is:
    \begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        q_t(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) &= \prod_{i=1}^L q_t(x_{t,i} \mid x_{0,i}), \\
        \text{where} \quad q_t(x_{t,i} | x_{0,i}) &= 
        \begin{cases}
            1 - \alpha(t) & \text{if } x_{t,i} = x_{0,i}, \\
            \alpha(t)    & \text{if } x_{t,i} = \texttt{[MASK]}, \\
            0            & \text{otherwise},
        \end{cases}
    \end{split}
    \label{eq:forward}
    \end{equation}
    where $\alpha: [0,1] \to [0,1]$ is a monotonically increasing masking schedule with $\alpha(0) = 0$ and $\alpha(1) = 1$. For example, one may use $\alpha(t)=t$.

    The reverse (denoising) model is parameterized by a neural network $f_\theta$ that produces token logits conditioned on the masked sequence $\mathbf{x}_t$ and time $t$. The training objective minimizes the reconstruction loss at masked positions:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{\substack{t \sim \mathcal{U}[0,1] \\ \mathbf{x}_0 \sim p_{\text{data}} \\ \mathbf{x}_t \sim q_t(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}} \left[ \sum_{i=1}^L \mathbf{1}\!\left[x_{t,i} = \texttt{[MASK]}\right] \log p_\theta(x_{0,i} \,|\, \mathbf{x}_t, t) \right]
        \label{eq:loss}
    \end{equation}
    where
    \begin{equation}
        p_\theta(x_{0,i} \,|\, \mathbf{x}_t, t)
        =
        \left[\mathrm{softmax}([f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t)]_i)\right]_{x_{0,i}}
    \end{equation}
    is the predicted probability of the clean token $x_{0,i}$ at position $i$.

    Unlike autoregressive models that generate tokens sequentially, DLLMs generate text through an iterative denoising process over discrete timesteps. Let $1=t_T>\cdots>t_1>t_0=0$ be a discretization of $[0,1]$. Starting from $\mathbf{x}_{t_T}$, typically a fully masked sequence, the model progressively reduces the masking level. At each step $k$, the model estimates $p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \,|\, \mathbf{x}_{t_k}, t_k)$, and $\mathbf{x}_{t_{k-1}}$ is obtained by updating a subset of masked positions according to the decoding schedule. This non-autoregressive generation enables parallel token prediction and global context awareness during synthesis.

    \subsection{Integrated Gradients and Diagonal Blindness}

    Integrated Gradients (IG) \cite{sundararajan2017axiomatic} is a feature-attribution method for a differentiable scalar function $F:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$. Given an input $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^D$ and a baseline $\mathbf{x}'\in\mathbb{R}^D$, the standard IG attribution for coordinate $j$ is defined by
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{IG}_j(F;\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')
        =
        (x_j-x_j')\int_0^1
        \frac{\partial F\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}' + \alpha(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')\bigr)}{\partial x_j}\,d\alpha.
        \label{eq:ig_standard}
    \end{equation}
    This is precisely the straight-line, or Aumann-Shapley, path from $\mathbf{x}'$ to $\mathbf{x}$. In the special case $\mathbf{x}'=\mathbf{0}_D$ and $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{1}_D$, \eqref{eq:ig_standard} reduces to
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{IG}_j(F;\mathbf{1}_D,\mathbf{0}_D)
        =
        \int_0^1 \frac{\partial F(\alpha \mathbf{1}_D)}{\partial x_j}\,d\alpha,
        \label{eq:ig_diagonal}
    \end{equation}
    which only probes gradients along the synchronized diagonal
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta=\{\alpha\mathbf{1}_D:\alpha\in[0,1]\}\subset[0,1]^D.
    \end{equation}

    This immediately yields a blindness phenomenon for diagonal-path IG: if an interaction term is invisible on $\Delta$, then straight-line IG cannot detect it even when it is nonzero away from the diagonal.

    \begin{theorem}[Diagonal blindness of straight-line IG]
    \label{thm:diag_blind}
    Let $H:[0,1]^D\to\mathbb{R}$ be continuously differentiable with
    \begin{equation}
        \frac{\partial H(\alpha\mathbf{1}_D)}{\partial x_j}=0
        \qquad
        \text{for all }\alpha\in[0,1],\ j\in[D].
        \label{eq:ig_blind_condition}
    \end{equation}
    Then
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{IG}_j(H;\mathbf{1}_D,\mathbf{0}_D)
        =0
        \qquad
        \text{for all }j\in[D],
        \label{eq:ig_blind_result}
    \end{equation}
    regardless of the magnitude of $\partial H/\partial x_j$ away from the diagonal $\Delta$.
    \end{theorem}

    \begin{proof}
    Substituting $H$ into \eqref{eq:ig_diagonal} gives
    \[
    \mathrm{IG}_j(H;\mathbf{1}_D,\mathbf{0}_D)
    =
    \int_0^1 \frac{\partial H(\alpha\mathbf{1}_D)}{\partial x_j}\,d\alpha
    =0,
    \]
    where the last equality follows from \eqref{eq:ig_blind_condition}.
    \end{proof}

    \paragraph{Example.}
    Consider $D=2$ with base function $F(g_1,g_2)=g_1+g_2$ and interaction $H(g_1,g_2)=(g_1-g_2)^3$. Since $\partial_1 H=3(g_1-g_2)^2$ and $\partial_2 H=-3(g_1-g_2)^2$ both vanish on $g_1=g_2$, condition~\eqref{eq:ig_blind_condition} is satisfied. Set $J=F+H$. Because $H(\mathbf{0}_2)=H(\mathbf{1}_2)=0$, the total change $J(\mathbf{1}_2)-J(\mathbf{0}_2)=2$ is the same as for $F$ alone, yet the per-coordinate picture changes dramatically: $J(1,0)=2$ (head~1 alone achieves the full target performance) while $J(0,1)=0$ (head~2 alone achieves nothing). Head~1 is clearly more important. However, on the diagonal $\partial_1 J(\alpha,\alpha)=\partial_2 J(\alpha,\alpha)=1$, so straight-line IG gives $\mathrm{IG}_1=\mathrm{IG}_2=1$, treating both heads as equally important. The asymmetric interaction $(g_1-g_2)^3$ is identically zero on $g_1=g_2$, so the diagonal path never encounters the configurations in which head~1 dominates.

    This limitation motivates moving beyond a single synchronized path when IG is applied to structured attribution problems. In the sequel, we instantiate IG in attention-head gate space and then generalize from the standard straight-line path to a family of paths better suited to asynchronous head interactions.
